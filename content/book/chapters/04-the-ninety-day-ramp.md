The first ninety days are where selection becomes either leverage or debt. A company learns whether its filter found the right raw material, whether its training created usable habits, and whether the manager can turn effort into performance without drowning the new hire in vague encouragement.

Ramp should be designed as a feedback system. The hire is learning the job, but the company is also learning whether its process predicted reality.

## Days 1–30: make effort legible

The first month should reveal preparation, note quality, follow-through, and response to repetition. A new seller does not need to sound senior. They need to show that practice is changing them.

Managers should look for visible deltas: cleaner questions, tighter summaries, better calendar discipline, and a growing ability to explain what happened after a call.

## Days 31–60: convert feedback into habits

The middle of ramp is where energy either becomes an operating system or stays as enthusiasm. The best hires begin to diagnose their own bottlenecks. They know which objection unsettles them. They bring call clips. They ask for narrower coaching.

This is also where partner context matters. A behavior that wins in one sales motion can create drag in another. The ramp plan should translate general sales fundamentals into the partner’s actual floor.

## Days 61–90: decide with clarity

By the end of ninety days, the team should be able to separate activity from progress. Booked meetings matter. So do qualified conversations, clean handoffs, and the judgment behind them.

The decision should be explicit: expand responsibility, adjust the support system, or admit that the signal is not strong enough. Clarity protects the partner, the candidate, and the next cohort.
