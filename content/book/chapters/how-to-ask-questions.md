Most salespeople ask bad questions.

They ask what they think they are supposed to ask. They ask the same questions to everyone. They ask "what is your biggest challenge?" and the person gives a one word answer and they are stuck.

A good question moves the conversation forward.

There is a structure to this.

In the beginning, you ask consultative questions. You are trying to understand what is going on beneath the surface.

They know they have a problem but they do not fully understand the dimensions of it or the cost of it. Your questions help them articulate it.

A bad consultative question sounds like "are you happy with your current situation?" They will just say yes or no.

A good consultative question sounds like "what has been the hardest part of dealing with that?" This forces them to think. It forces them to access the emotional reality.

The pattern is this.

Start broad. What has been going on with your career? Then get specific. What about that has been frustrating? Then get emotional. How has that affected your confidence? Then get honest. What do you think is stopping you?

Listen more than you talk. When they answer, sit with it. Ask a follow up. Most people are not used to being truly listened to in a sales conversation. When you listen, it changes the dynamic. They feel heard. They open up.

People do not buy based on how bad the problem is objectively. They buy based on how much they feel the problem. And they only feel it when they have articulated it themselves.

If you tell them their situation is bad, they will defend it. If they tell themselves their situation is bad and articulate the cost, they are ready to solve it.

The second type of question happens in the middle. These are pattern questions.

You are trying to understand how they think about the problem. What do they believe about what is possible?

If someone says "I am not smart enough to make the jump to tech," you do not say "actually you are, lots of people have."

You ask "where do you think that belief comes from?" They tell you.

Then: "has that belief been tested? Have you tried to learn something hard and failed or is this something you have just assumed?"

These questions reveal what is stopping them.

The third type happens near the close. These are confirmation questions.

"Does it make sense to you why this would help?" or "If this worked the way I am describing, would that change things?"

These move people closer to commitment incrementally.

The progression matters.

Consult to pattern to confirmation. If you skip it or jump around, it does not work.

You are gathering information early. You are understanding their beliefs in the middle. You are moving toward commitment at the end.
