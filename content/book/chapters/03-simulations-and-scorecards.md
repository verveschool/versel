Simulations are useful because they make the work visible. They do not need to mimic a full sales cycle. They need to isolate the moments where the role regularly breaks: opening a cold conversation, clarifying pain, handling an objection, asking for commitment, and summarizing next steps.

A generic role-play is better than an unstructured interview, but it is still too blunt. The strongest simulations are partner-specific. They reflect the market, sales motion, buyer profile, and emotional texture of the job the candidate is actually entering.

## Designing the exercise

Start with the partner’s real constraints. Is the role outbound-heavy? Does it require high-volume recovery? Are buyers technical, skeptical, hurried, or price-sensitive? Does success depend on speed, patience, executive presence, or relentless note hygiene?

Then build the simulation around the behavior that matters most. A candidate should not be scored on theatrical confidence if the job primarily requires disciplined discovery. They should not be punished for being concise if the buyer rewards brevity.

## Writing the scorecard

A scorecard is a promise to the candidate and the partner. It says: these are the behaviors we care about, and this is how we will judge them.

Good scorecards separate evidence from interpretation. “Asked two clarifying questions before pitching” is evidence. “Seems strategic” is interpretation. Both may be useful, but only one can be reviewed cleanly later.

## Keeping the loop alive

The simulation is not finished when the offer is made. Its predictions should be compared against ramp data. If the exercise overvalued confidence, fix it. If it missed resilience, add a moment that reveals recovery. If one partner environment rewards a behavior another partner does not, split the filter.

The system improves when it remembers. Every cohort should make the next scorecard sharper.
