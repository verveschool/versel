One of the things that separates good closers from great closers is speed.

Not speed of talking. Speed of diagnosis.

Great closers don't have to think about what to do next. They know. They've seen the pattern before. They recognize the person in front of them and they adjust automatically.

This is called unconscious competence. You've practiced something so much that you don't have to consciously think about it anymore.

When you first learn these frameworks, you have to think about every step.

Is this the consultative phase or the pattern phase? Which certainty gap is this? Do I ask another question or move to presenting?

All of that conscious processing slows you down. You sound scripted. You sound like you're checking boxes. The person can feel that you're being methodical instead of conversational.

As you practice, something changes.

You've seen enough conversations that you can consult in your sleep.

Because you know where the gaps are before they articulate them. You know which questions will unlock the information. You know when to push and when to give space.

This is the skill that makes you dangerous.

Here's how you build it.

You develop your internal database of pattern recognition.

You start noticing that there are patterns to the types of people who buy.

There's a type who is stuck and knows it and wants out.

There's a type who is complacent and probably won't buy.

There's a type who is greedy and will buy anything that promises fast money.

There's a type who is skeptical of everything and needs overwhelming evidence.

Once you know the patterns, you adjust.

With the stuck person, you validate their instinct that something needs to change.

With the complacent person, you try to amplify the pain.

With the greedy person, you focus on the financial upside.

With the skeptical person, you provide evidence and don't get offended by their doubt.

Here's what you're doing when you learn patterns. You're building a model of different buying profiles.

You're learning that not everyone buys for the same reason. Not everyone moves at the same pace. Not everyone needs the same certainties in the same order.

The person who understands patterns sees a prospect in the first 90 seconds and knows what they're dealing with.

They know which questions will work. They know which objections are coming. They know what the person is afraid of underneath the surface.

This is where the leverage is. Not in having better techniques. In having faster pattern recognition.

Here's how you build this.

You record your calls. You listen to them. You notice patterns in what works and what doesn't.

You notice the calls where you closed and the calls where you didn't. What was different?

You start seeing that certain questions work with certain types. You start seeing that certain objections come from certain fears.

You track this. You build your own script. A flexible set of patterns you can lean on.

You also get feedback from people better than you. You listen to their calls. You watch how they diagnose. You watch how they adjust. You steal their moves and make them your own.

Over time, what was conscious becomes unconscious.

You don't have to think about whether to ask a diagnostic question. You just ask it.

You don't have to think about which certainty gap you're dealing with. You just know.

And that's when you start getting fast. That's when you start closing more deals.

That's when the whole thing becomes effortless.

But you have to practice enough to get there. You can't just read this book and hope it happens.

You have to get on calls. You have to bomb some. You have to get feedback. You have to adjust.

You have to do it again and again until it becomes automatic.

The progression goes like this.

First you're conscious and incompetent. You don't know what you're doing and you know it.

Then you're conscious and competent. You know what you're doing but you have to think about it.

Then you're unconscious and competent. You know what you're doing and you don't have to think about it. You're just flowing.

That last stage is where mastery lives.
