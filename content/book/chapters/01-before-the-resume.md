Every sales hiring process begins before the resume arrives. The market has already decided which candidates look obvious, which candidates look unfinished, and which candidates never enter the funnel because no one translated their ambition into a role they could imagine doing well.

The mistake is to treat the resume as the first source of truth. For early-career sellers, it is often the least useful artifact. It compresses context, hides pressure behavior, and rewards people who already know how to describe themselves in the language of corporate certainty.

## The hidden market

The hidden market is not mysterious. It is made of people with high social energy, unusual follow-through, and an appetite for visible progress who have not yet been told that sales is a craft. They may be working service jobs, running campus organizations, selling informally, competing in sports, or helping family businesses survive.

Their signal appears in motion. They reply quickly. They ask what good looks like. They recover after awkward conversations. They keep promises that were small enough for other people to ignore.

## What changes when you scout earlier

When a company scouts before the resume, it stops asking whether a candidate already looks like a sales hire and starts asking whether the candidate can become one under the right pressure. That shift matters because early sales roles are often won by learning velocity, not polish.

The practical question becomes: can we observe the behaviors that predict trust before we sell the candidate or the company a fantasy?

## A better first filter

A better first filter is simple and demanding. Give the candidate a real prompt. Watch how they clarify ambiguity. Ask them to write down what they heard. Introduce a constraint. See whether they protect the relationship while still moving the conversation forward.

This does not remove judgment from hiring. It makes judgment more honest. The goal is not to find perfect people. The goal is to identify raw commercial behavior early enough that training can compound it.
