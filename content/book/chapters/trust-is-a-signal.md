---
title: "Trust is a signal"
description: "Trust is not built by remembering their dog's name. It is built by being entirely willing to walk away."
order: "8"
part: "The mechanics"
---

Most people think trust is built through likeability and consistency.

Show up on time. Remember their name. Be personable.

That's not trust. That's basic professionalism.

Real trust is when someone believes you care whether this is the right decision for them more than you care about closing.

Here are the signals that build it.

Signal one: competence

They need to believe you know what you're talking about.

This doesn't mean you know everything. It means you ask intelligent questions that reveal you understand their world. It means you can reference patterns you've seen.

It means you understand the options and why you're suggesting this one.

When you ask a smart question about their situation, their respect goes up.

When you reference something they said earlier and connect it to something else, they realize you're listening.

When you say "I've talked to hundreds of people in your situation and here's what I notice," they believe you because the specificity is credible.

Signal two: listening

When someone talks, can they tell you're hearing them? Are your follow up questions shaped by what they just said?

Or are you repeating something you memorized.

People feel the difference immediately because listening responds to their words and pretending falls back on recall.

When you're listening, you sit with what they said. You think through what they meant underneath it.

You ask the next question because it's the only one that makes sense.

Signal three: being upfront about limitations

Most salespeople think mentioning a limitation will kill the deal.

Actually the opposite is true.

When you're honest about what your product can't do, it signals that you won't squeeze money from someone who is not the right fit.

It signals that you care about outcomes.

"This program requires effort from you. If you want a shortcut, this isn't it."

When you say that, the people ready to work lean in. The people looking for magic go elsewhere.

The deal quality goes up.

Signal four: willing to say no

If someone is genuinely not a good fit, you tell them.

You refer them somewhere else.

This is the ultimate signal. It means your incentives are aligned with theirs.

The moment you're willing to walk away, you're not desperate. The moment you're not desperate, you don't sound desperate.

People respect that. They're more likely to buy from you when they know you would turn them down.

Signal five: alignment between words and actions

If you say you care but you're always in a rush, there's a mismatch.

If you say you're here to help but you're pushing toward close before they're ready, there's a mismatch.

People unconsciously track whether what you say matches how you act.

Mismatches erode personal certainty. Alignment builds it.

Signal six: genuine curiosity

When you ask questions, does it feel like you want to understand or does it feel like you're checking boxes?

Genuine curiosity is hard to fake.

You have to care about the answer. You have to find the person interesting.

The thing that separates great closers from okay ones is not technique. It's integrity.

They don't want to sell to someone who would make a bad decision.

They believe that saying no sometimes is more important than hitting a number.

They care about outcomes more than transactions.

Once you have that orientation, everything becomes easier.

Your questions come from a place.

Your listen with deep care.

Your assessment of fit is accurate.

The person can feel that you're an ally in them making a good decision.

Personal certainty forms because they sense you would protect them from a bad choice even if it costs you the sale.
